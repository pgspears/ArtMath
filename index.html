      How a computer paints with math

      A screen is a grid of tiny colored dots. Each dot’s color is three numbers: how much red, green and blue. Making a picture comes down to one question, asked for every dot: what color should this be?

      A painter answers that question with a brush and a camera answers it with light. Here a rule written in code answers it. A simple rule such as “darker the further from the center” gives a spotlight. The rules on this page come from fractal geometry, chaos theory, chemistry, Einstein’s gravity and the geometry of four dimensions. The artist chooses the rule; the rule makes the picture.

      Motion comes from time. Every rule also takes the moment as an input, so the same rule gives a slightly different picture a thirtieth of a second later. Some scenes are formulas that can be worked out for any moment directly. Others are simulations: a small virtual world of chemicals, pendulums or stars, nudged forward one tiny step at a time.

      All of it runs on your graphics card, the part of a computer built to do the same small calculation for millions of pixels at once. That is why a deep fractal zoom, millions of pendulums and a black hole bending light can all play live in a web page.

      This is not AI image generation

      AI image and video generators learn from millions of existing pictures and produce new ones that statistically resemble them. Procedural art works the other way round: there is no training, no dataset and no prompt. Every rule is written out, and every pixel can be traced back to a line of math you can read.

      An old idea, seen fresh

      Artists have made pictures from rules and computers for more than sixty years. Georg Nees exhibited computer drawings in Stuttgart in February 1965, one of the first shows of its kind. Vera Molnár was making art from simple rules on paper before she began working with computers in 1968. The demoscene, a worldwide community that makes real-time art from compact code, is now recognized as cultural heritage in several countries. Shadertoy, launched in 2013, is a home for images written entirely as math that runs in the browser, like the ones on this page.

      How it was made

      The code behind these animations was written by Claude, an AI assistant, working from a brief to show the limits of code-generated imagery. That is different from AI image generation: no AI drew a single frame. The pictures come from your own computer running readable instructions, the same kind a human programmer writes.

      Everything is open to inspection. Each scene is one short JavaScript file with its shader inside, and every color, camera move and musical note can be traced to a specific line. The score is synthesized live too: turn on sound and every note is calculated in your browser.

      The same scenes were first written in C and rendered into two films on an ordinary two-core computer. This page runs them in real time instead.

      Every frame and every note computed from mathematics. Code written with Claude; no image or video AI. Keyboard: space to pause, arrow keys to change scene, F for full screen.
